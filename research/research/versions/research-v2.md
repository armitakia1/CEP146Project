## Moffatt v. Air Canada (Canada, 2024)

Sources: Law360 Canada (2024); Foster & Company (n.d.); the decision, 2024 BCCRT 149.

The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal decided this case in February 2024. Jake Moffatt asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares after a family member died. The chatbot said the fare could be requested after the flight, which was not the airline's actual policy. Air Canada argued the chatbot was effectively a separate entity responsible for itself. The tribunal rejected that, said the chatbot is part of the website, and held the company responsible for everything on it.

The legal test was negligent misrepresentation: a duty of care, a misleading statement made without reasonable care, reasonable reliance, and damage. Air Canada was ordered to pay $812.02 including interest and fees. We still need to confirm that figure in the decision. This was a small claims tribunal, so we should not overstate its weight as precedent, and we need to confirm how binding tribunal decisions are.

## Benavides v. Tesla (United States)

Sources: Reuters (2025, August 26); AP (2025); Reuters (2026, February 20); Tesla, Inc. (2026); Washington Legal Foundation (2026).

The crash happened in 2019 in Key Largo, Florida. The driver, George McGee, was using Tesla Autopilot, looked away for a dropped phone and hit a parked SUV. Naibel Benavides Leon died, and Dillon Angulo was seriously injured. A federal jury in Miami found Tesla 33% responsible and the driver 67% in August 2025. Compensatory damages came to $129 million, of which Tesla owes its 33% share, about $42.6 million. The jury added $200 million in punitive damages against Tesla, for roughly $243 million in total.

Tesla argued that the driver alone was to blame and the car was not defective, and it had turned down a $60 million settlement before trial. Judge Bloom refused to overturn the verdict in February 2026, and Tesla filed a notice of appeal on March 16, 2026. In July 2026 the Washington Legal Foundation filed an amicus brief arguing the punitive award violates Florida law and due process. That is an advocacy position. The appeal is pending, so the verdict is not final, and we need to check the docket again before recording.

## Uber self-driving crash (United States, 2018)

Sources: Reuters (2020); WAMC (2020); Reuters (2023).

In March 2018 an Uber test vehicle in autonomous mode killed a pedestrian, Elaine Herzberg, in Tempe, Arizona. These notes say prosecutors decided in March 2019 that Uber was not criminally liable. The backup driver, Rafaela Vasquez, was charged with negligent homicide in 2020, and police said she was looking at her phone. In 2023 she pleaded guilty to a reduced charge of endangerment and received three years of supervised probation. The NTSB named her failure to monitor the road as the main cause but also criticized Uber for not managing the risk of operator inattention. The notes also say Uber settled with the victim's family. Several of these details were later found to be unconfirmed (see v4).

## Mobley v. Workday (United States, AI hiring)

Sources: Proskauer (2025); Civil Rights Litigation Clearinghouse (n.d.).

The plaintiff says Workday's AI screening tools discriminated on the basis of race, age and disability. Workday is a software vendor, not the employer, so the plaintiff argues it is liable as the employers' agent. In July 2024 the court let the claims go forward on that theory. In May 2025 it conditionally certified an age-discrimination collective that could include a very large number of applicants. We do not know the current status. These are allegations and procedural rulings, not a finding that Workday discriminated.

## Garcia v. Character Technologies (United States)

Sources: Courthouse News (2025); Branum (2026); Bloomberg Law (2026).

This is a wrongful-death suit after the suicide of a 14-year-old, Sewell Setzer III, following long conversations with a chatbot. In May 2025 Judge Conway declined to dismiss on First Amendment grounds. She was not prepared to say at that stage that the chatbot's output is speech, and product liability and negligence claims went ahead. The case settled in January 2026, and similar suits continue. Character.AI also banned open-ended chats for under-18 users, but we need to confirm the year (probably 2025). The topic is sensitive, so we should handle it carefully in the video or choose another example.

## EU law

Sources: Taylor Wessing (2025); LexisNexis (2025); Bird & Bird (2025); Wiggin (n.d.); Covington (2026).

The new Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 came into force on December 8, 2024, and treats software and AI as products. It keeps no-fault liability, so a victim does not need to prove negligence, only the defect, the damage and the link between them. It can also reach faulty software updates and cybersecurity weaknesses. It applies to products placed on the market after December 9, 2026, which is under two months away. The separate AI Liability Directive, proposed in 2022, was withdrawn by the Commission in February 2025 because no agreement was expected.

## Canada

Sources: Montreal AI Ethics Institute (n.d.); Baker McKenzie (n.d.).

The proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act was part of Bill C-27 and died when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025. There is no dedicated federal AI law, so AI harms are handled through existing law, such as negligent misrepresentation in *Moffatt*. ## How the version 1 assumptions held up

1. Courts will not treat AI as a person: supported by *Moffatt*. We found no case where a court did.
2. The deployer is responsible for what its AI tells customers: supported by *Moffatt*.
3. Blame is split between company and human: supported by *Benavides* (33% and 67%) and the Uber case.
4. Canada has no dedicated AI law: supported, but we need an official source.
5. The EU is ahead on written rules: partly supported. The EU also dropped its AI-specific liability proposal, and the product liability update does most of the work.
