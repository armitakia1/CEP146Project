## Summary

In every case I found, responsibility landed on people and companies, never on the AI. In *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, a tribunal rejected the airline's argument that its chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own statements. Who pays depends on the legal theory: misrepresentation by the company that deployed the tool, a defective product, or a failure to supervise. The EU is the clearest exception to the "old law, new technology" pattern, because it rewrote its product liability law to cover software and AI by name.

## What "unintended" means in law

The EU Directive speaks to this directly. Recital 32 says a manufacturer that designs a product able to develop unexpected behaviour stays liable for harm it causes. Article 7(2)(c) says a court deciding whether a product is defective must consider its ability to keep learning after sale. So "nobody planned for the AI to do that" is not a defence under the new EU rules.

## Cases

### Air Canada's chatbot (Canada, 2024)

Jake Moffatt's grandmother died on November 11, 2022. He used the chatbot on Air Canada's website to ask about bereavement fares, and it told him he could apply for the reduced rate after travelling. The airline's policy didn't allow that (paras. 13 to 17).

Air Canada argued it wasn't liable for what its agents, servants or representatives said, including the chatbot. The tribunal read this as claiming the chatbot was a separate legal entity. Tribunal Member Christopher C. Rivers called it "a remarkable submission" and held that the chatbot is part of the airline's website (para. 27). The tribunal applied negligent misrepresentation and ordered Air Canada to pay $812.02: $650.88 in damages, $36.14 in interest and $125 in fees (para. 44).

It's a small claims tribunal decision, so according to Cox & Palmer (n.d.) it doesn't bind courts in later cases.

### Tesla Autopilot: *Benavides v. Tesla* (United States)

On April 25, 2019, in Key Largo, Florida, George McGee was driving a Model S with Autopilot on at about 62 mph. He bent down to look for a dropped phone and hit an SUV parked on the shoulder. Naibel Benavides Leon died and Dillon Angulo was seriously injured (Stempel, 2026).

In August 2025 a federal jury found Tesla 33% responsible. Tesla's share was $19.5 million to the Benavides estate and $23.1 million to Angulo, plus $200 million in punitive damages, about $243 million in total. McGee had settled earlier (Stempel, 2026; Condon & Fischer, 2025). Judge Beth Bloom refused to overturn the verdict on February 20, 2026, and Tesla filed its notice of appeal on March 16, 2026 (Tesla, Inc., 2026). The verdict isn't final.

### Uber self-driving test crash (United States, 2018 to 2023)

An Uber test vehicle in autonomous mode killed Elaine Herzberg in Tempe, Arizona. The NTSB blamed the backup driver for not watching the road but also faulted Uber's development decisions. Prosecutors charged the driver with negligent homicide in 2020, and she later pleaded guilty (Reuters, 2020; Reuters, 2023). I've only confirmed the headlines of those two articles so far. Earlier notes said prosecutors declined to charge Uber and that Uber settled with the family. I couldn't confirm either, so I took them out.

### Workday hiring lawsuit (United States, ongoing)

Applicants say an AI screening tool discriminated against them. The court let the claims go forward against the vendor on the theory that it acts as the employers' agent, and conditionally certified a large age-discrimination group. These are allegations and procedural rulings, not findings. I haven't yet read the sources closely enough to rely on this section.

### Character.AI (United States)

A court refused at an early stage to treat a chatbot's output as protected speech, and the case later settled. I haven't yet rechecked the details. The case involves a child's death, so I'm considering a different example for the video.

## The law in writing

### European Union

I read this from the Directive itself. Directive (EU) 2024/2853 was signed on October 23, 2024 and entered into force on December 8, 2024. It applies to products placed on the market after December 9, 2026 (art. 2(1)). Software counts as a product (art. 4(1)), and recital 13 says AI system providers are treated as manufacturers. Liability is no-fault (recital 6), though the injured person still has to prove the damage, the defect and the link between them (art. 10(1)). A manufacturer can't escape liability by saying a software problem appeared after sale, if the problem is within its control (art. 11(2)).

The Directive covers death, personal injury, property damage and loss of private data (art. 6). Recital 24 says pure economic loss and discrimination don't trigger liability by themselves, so a loss like Moffatt's would need a different legal route.

The separate AI Liability Directive was withdrawn in 2025, but I haven't confirmed the date or the reasons.

### Canada

The proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act didn't pass, so there's no AI-specific federal liability law. I still need an official source on current plans.

### United States

I didn't find a single federal AI liability law. Cases use product liability, negligence, anti-discrimination and criminal law.

## Open questions

What happens when nobody could have predicted what the AI did? The EU keeps the manufacturer liable, but I found no equivalent written rule for the US or Canada, and I still need a peer-reviewed source on the "responsibility gap." The *Benavides* appeal could change the biggest verdict. The EU Directive leaves out pure economic loss and discrimination, so some harms have no clear route to compensation.
